# Worked Example — Onboarding Retro, Four Structures in 100 Minutes

Context: a 14-person team (support, sales, product, onboarding specialists, one director) is six weeks into a new customer onboarding flow. Completion is stuck at 61 percent, the weekly retro keeps producing the same three complaints, and the director tends to open with a diagnosis. The facilitator runs four structures in sequence, choosing each one for the state the previous one produced.

## Pre-work

- Question on the wall: "What makes new customers stall in their first week, and what will we change?"
- Constraint stated up front: no new headcount this quarter; the team controls the welcome sequence, the handoff checklist, and the help content.
- Decision rights named: the director decides budget; the team decides process changes; the onboarding lead owns the customer-facing changes.
- Artifact target: a stop list, a set of owned first moves, and one portfolio decision.

## 09:00 — 1-2-4-All (15 min): what is actually happening

The facilitator asks: "Where exactly do customers stall, and what do we each see from our position?" Silent minute, then pairs, then quads of four (three quads plus one pair).

What the harvest shows:

- Support: the same three questions arrive on day two, so the welcome email is not answering them.
- Sales: handoffs arrive without the promise that was made on the call; the customer expects a call that never comes.
- Onboarding: the checklist is completed by the specialist, not the customer, so completion is a staff metric.
- Product: the in-app tour fires before the account is configured, so customers skip it.

The director's opening diagnosis ("customers don't read email") is not in the harvest. The facilitator reads it back as one hypothesis among four, without privileging it.

Why this structure: the group is larger than four, the director speaks first by habit, and the team needs breadth before convergence.

## 09:15 — TRIZ (40 min): what are we doing that guarantees failure

Framing: "Assume we want every new customer to stall. What would we do?"

Worst-result list (selected entries, verbatim):

- Make the customer talk to a different person at every step.
- Promise a call on the sales call and never schedule it.
- Send a welcome email that lists features instead of the first three actions.
- Ask the customer to fill in the same information twice.
- Complete the checklist on the customer's behalf so nobody notices they are stuck.
- Fire the product tour before the account works.

Vote: the group picks five. Then the turn: "Which of these are we already doing?" Every one of the five is at least partially true. The room goes quiet; the onboarding lead says the checklist issue out loud for the first time.

Stop list, with owners and replacements:

| Stop | Owner | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Specialist completes the checklist | Onboarding lead | Customer completes it with a two-minute walkthrough; specialist reviews, does not fill |
| Sales promises a call without scheduling it | Sales lead | Call booked in the handoff form before the deal closes |
| Welcome email lists features | Lifecycle owner | Email names the first three actions and links to one page |
| Tour fires at signup | Product | Tour triggers after account configuration, with a skip option |

Why this structure: the group already knew the dysfunction but had never named it collectively; TRIZ lets the room accuse the system rather than each other.

## 10:00 — 15% Solutions (25 min): what can each person start now

The constraint is restated: no budget, no new hires. Each person writes their own 15 percent. In threes, they sharpen with two questions: "What would make that easier?" and "What is the first 30 minutes?"

Sample actions:

- Support: rewrite the three day-two answers into the welcome email draft this week (first 30 minutes: pull the ten most recent tickets).
- Sales: add the scheduled-call field to the handoff form and test it on the next three deals.
- Onboarding: run the walkthrough with two customers and time it.
- Product: change the tour trigger behind a flag for the next cohort.
- Director: remove the completion metric from the weekly report and replace it with time-to-first-value, which is within their authority.

The shared blocker that emerges — the handoff form lives in a tool the team cannot edit — is logged as an escalation item, not used as a reason to wait.

Why this structure: the TRIZ stop list created a "we have no authority" reflex. 15% Solutions converts the stop list into actions inside existing discretion.

## 10:25 — Ecocycle Planning (35 min, abbreviated)

The facilitator brings a pre-drawn figure-eight and asks the group to place onboarding-related activities: the welcome email, the in-app tour, the handoff checklist, the weekly retro, the help centre, the customer community, the Q3 webinar series.

Placement:

- **Maturity:** welcome email, help centre, handoff checklist, weekly retro.
- **Gestation / birth:** the walkthrough experiment, the new tour trigger.
- **Renewal, unresourced:** customer community, Q3 webinar series — both named repeatedly, neither owned.
- **Creative destruction candidates:** the weekly retro as currently run (it keeps reproducing the same three complaints), and the Q3 webinar series, which consumes two people's time and has no attendance data.

Decisions:

1. Stop the weekly retro in its current form; replace it with a biweekly review of the stop list and the time-to-first-value metric. Owner: director. Review in four weeks.
2. Pause the Q3 webinar series and release the two people to the customer community experiment for one cohort. Owner: director and lifecycle owner.
3. Move the walkthrough experiment from gestation into a two-week pilot with a named success signal: five customers complete configuration without a specialist filling anything.

Why this structure: the portfolio was crowded and everything looked mature. The lifecycle map made stopping a legitimate move rather than an admission of failure.

## 10:55 — Close (5 min)

Read-back:

- Stop list: four practices, named owners, replacements.
- First moves: five 15 percent actions with dates inside the next seven days.
- Portfolio: one stop, one pause, one renewal pilot with a two-week window.
- Escalation: handoff form edit access, owner director, decision by Friday.
- Dissent: one specialist says the walkthrough adds staff time; recorded as a risk against the pilot, not resolved by majority.

## What made the sequence work

- Each structure produced a different artifact — themes, stop list, actions, portfolio decisions — so nothing was repeated.
- The director's early diagnosis was absorbed into 1-2-4-All rather than allowed to frame the room.
- TRIZ named the collective behaviour without personal blame, which made the stop list actionable.
- 15% Solutions ran after TRIZ, not before, because the group needed to see the system before claiming its discretionary space.
- Ecocycle ran last because stopping requires the group to have already named the cost of the status quo.

## What would have gone wrong

- Running 1-2-4-All as a "quick round" without a silent minute → director's frame survives.
- Skipping the "which are we already doing?" turn in TRIZ → the room enjoys the joke and changes nothing.
- Treating the 15 percent actions as commitments without dates → the retro ends in enthusiasm.
- Running Ecocycle first → the group places everything in maturity and defends the portfolio.
- Four structures without harvests → four conversations, no artifact.

Numbers and quotes in this example are illustrative, not data from a real organisation.
