# Forcing Techniques

Forcing means extracting properties and making them constrain the problem. The first link is usually a cliché; the useful link is two or three properties deeper. Use these techniques in order, and stop when you have enough material — not when the first idea arrives.

## 1. Property inventory

List **8–10 literal properties** before asking what they mean. Sweep these categories:

| Category | Ask | Example: compost |
|---|---|---|
| Material / parts | What is it made of? What are its pieces? | Dead plant matter, water, air, microbes |
| Affordances | What does it let you do or forbid? | Contains waste; cannot be rushed; needs turning |
| Structural | What is its shape, boundary, layers? | A bin with a lid and a base layer |
| Temporal | What is its lifecycle, cadence, latency? | Months to mature; periodic turning |
| Relational | Who operates it, who benefits, what does it separate? | Household feeds it; garden consumes it |
| Quantity / ratio | What must be balanced or proportioned? | Carbon-to-nitrogen ratio; moisture |
| Failure | How does it fail, and what does failure look like? | Anaerobic smell, pests, stalled decay |
| Symbolic (last) | What is it associated with? | Rot, return, renewal |

Rules:

- **True first.** If a property is false, it cannot force anything. Mark uncertain knowledge as assumption and drop it from use.
- **Ten is a floor, not a target.** Stop when further properties are restatements.
- **Write the property, not the lesson.** "One-way" is a property; "gatekeeping" is an interpretation.
- Do the symbolic category **last** and use it sparingly; it is where clichés enter.

## 2. Property mapping

Build a table with one row per property. The middle column must describe what the property does *in the stimulus*; the right column is the forced move.

| Property | What it does in the stimulus | If the problem had this property… | Candidate change |
|---|---|---|---|
| One-way passage | Lets people through in one direction only; blocks return | Knowledge could only move ticket → article, never back | Closing a ticket requires a reusable answer to exist in the article |
| Ratchet | Advances in steps, cannot slip backward | Progress would persist instead of resetting each sprint | Each sprint's fixes are committed to the article before the next starts |
| Counting | Records every passage | Reuse would be measurable per article | Count article citations at ticket close |

Prompts to force the third column:

- **Magnitude:** what if it were 10× larger or smaller?
- **Direction:** what if the flow reversed, or became one-way?
- **Timing:** what if it were immediate or delayed by a month?
- **Ownership:** what if a different actor held it, or nobody did?
- **Cost:** what if it were free, or expensive enough to ration?
- **Visibility:** what if everyone could see it, or only one person could?
- **Prohibition:** what would this property forbid us from doing?

A candidate change that survives three prompts is usually more specific than the one you started with.

## 3. Analogy sprint

The four Synectics analogies give four different ways into the same stimulus. Run one round per analogy, about two minutes each, using a **different property** each time.

1. **Direct analogy** — "Our problem is like the stimulus because…" Complete the sentence with a causal clause, not a resemblance. *"Our help centre is like a compost bin because both stall when the material is unbalanced."*
2. **Personal analogy** — "If I were the stimulus inside this system, I would…" Take the stimulus's position literally: what would be easy, impossible, or exhausting? This surfaces incentives and friction a detached description misses.
3. **Symbolic analogy** — Compress the stimulus into a **contradictory phrase** or a verb: "slow gate", "counted trust", "deliberate rot". Then ask what in the problem could be described that way. Do not explain the phrase; use it as a constraint.
4. **Fantasy analogy** — Remove cost, physics, and authority: "If the stimulus could do anything…" Use it to find the desired effect, then strip the impossible part and keep the mechanism.

Record every sentence verbatim. The bad ones show where the obvious associations are; the good ones usually arrive in rounds three and four, after the inventory has been exhausted.

## 4. Steal the verb

Turn the stimulus into verbs and apply each to the problem:

> compost → bury, layer, turn, heat, wait, break down, feed
> turnstile → gate, ratchet, count, queue, separate, admit

For each verb, write "How could we ⟨verb⟩ ⟨part of the problem⟩?" A verb imports an action that a noun does not, and it is harder to make into a pun.

## Quality checks

Before moving to evaluation:

- **≥3 distinct properties used.** One property is a metaphor; three is a forcing.
- **≥1 non-obvious link.** If every link was predictable from the stimulus's name, you have not forced anything.
- **Every link names a mechanism.** "Because…" followed by a causal path, not a feeling.
- **No surface resemblance.** Shared vocabulary, shape, or color does not count.
- **The stimulus's properties remain accurate.** Check claims against the object or the concept's actual definition; a false property invalidates the link.

## Signs you are no longer forcing

- You are explaining the stimulus's meaning instead of using its properties.
- Every candidate change is a restatement of the baseline ideas.
- The links require the stimulus to be something it is not.
- You have a beautiful metaphor and no actor, rule, or measurement.
- The conversation has moved to the problem's usual solutions with the stimulus as decoration.

If forcing stalls, redraw the stimulus rather than pushing harder on a dead one. Forcing is cheap; defending a bad draw is expensive.
