# Provenance — Lineage, Attribution, and Evidence Limits

## The lineage

- **Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi (1804–1851)**, German mathematician, worked by inverting problems: to solve a difficult forward problem, formulate and solve its inverse. His working motto is reported as *"man muss immer umkehren"* — "one must always invert." The motto is widely quoted through Munger; it is not a formal theorem, and the mathematical technique and the reasoning habit are different things.
- **Charles T. Munger (1924–2023)**, investor, popularized the habit as "Invert, always invert." He attributed the phrase to Jacobi and applied it to business and life: instead of asking how to succeed, ask how to guarantee failure and then avoid it. Two frequently cited instances:
  - *"A Lesson on Elementary, Worldly Wisdom as It Relates to Investment Management and Business"* (USC Marriott School, 1994), which includes the instruction to invert and the example of asking what would ruin a business rather than what would grow it.
  - The 1986 Harvard School commencement address, later titled *"Prescription for Misery,"* which builds an explicit recipe for a miserable life — unreliability, learning only from your own experience, giving up at the first failure — and then recommends the reverse.
  Munger's related maxim: avoiding stupidity is easier than seeking brilliance.
- **Marcus Aurelius**, *Meditations* 5.20: *"The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way."* The Stoic practice behind it — *premeditatio malorum*, the premeditation of adversity (Seneca, *Letters*) — rehearses the unwanted outcome in advance. The modern method differs in output: the Stoics trained composure; inversion produces design principles.
- **Via negativa**: the apophatic tradition defines by negation, and Nassim Taleb (*Antifragile*, 2012) applies "via negativa" to knowledge and risk — knowing what to avoid is more robust than knowing what to do. This is the same epistemic preference inversion exploits, not a direct source of the workflow.
- **Scott Adams**, *How to Fail at Almost Everything and Still Win Big* (2013) and related writing, popularized the practical prompt "how would I guarantee failure?" as a way to find a success path by avoiding the failure recipe. Often summarized as: the fastest route to success is to remove the reliable routes to failure.

## What is original here

The following are workflow adaptations for this repository, not claims about the sources:

- The six-step process (intake, observable success definition, invert, cluster by mechanism, extract principles, probe).
- The two-direction frame (goal inversion and outcome inversion) and the ten-domain generation sweep.
- The clustering rules, coverage check, and mechanism-versus-trigger distinction.
- The principle template (rule, failure route, signal, owner/decision, trade-off) and its quality tests.
- The onboarding worked example, comparison tables, and handoff map.
- The offline `scripts/invert.py` prompt generator.

Attribute the core move — invert the question, guarantee failure, avoid the recipe — to Jacobi and Munger, with Adams as a popularizer. Attribute the elaborations above to this skill as adaptations.

## Evidence and limits

- There is **no controlled study of inversion as a decision method**. Its value rests on the asymmetry that failure conditions are easier to enumerate than success conditions, and on practitioner experience (Munger's, Adams', and others').
- Adjacent evidence: Mitchell, Russo, and Pennington (1989) found that **prospective hindsight** — imagining an event has already occurred — improved identification of reasons for future outcomes by about 30%, as cited by Gary Klein's premortem work. That study concerns reason generation under a declared failure; it does **not** validate inversion's clustering or principle-derivation steps, and the figure should not be transferred as an effect size for this method.
- The method is qualitative. It surfaces plausible mechanisms and design conditions; it does not estimate probabilities, discover unknown unknowns, or replace pilots, base rates, or formal risk analysis.
- Inversion has known biases of its own: it over-weights the dramatic and the recently experienced, and a group can converge on familiar risks. Independent generation, the domain sweep, and the coverage check are mitigations, not guarantees.
- A failure route is a **hypothesis**. Mark observed, inferred, and speculative routes, and do not cite this skill's example, clusters, or principles as evidence about your own context.
- Attribution caution: the Jacobi motto and Munger's uses are reported in secondary sources and collected talks; exact wording varies across editions and transcripts. Quote the idea, not a contested verbatim line.
