# Provenance, Lineage, and Evidence Limits

This skill is a synthesis. Its parts have recognizable ancestors; the specific six-step process, three-test taxonomy, and five reframe patterns are local adaptations, not canonical formulations.

## Primary lineage

**Edward de Bono — challenge as a deliberate operation.** De Bono treated "challenge" as a distinct thinking operation: identify the dominant idea behind a situation and question why it must be there, rather than accepting it as part of the landscape. This appears in his lateral-thinking and CoRT material (notably *Serious Creativity*, 1992, and the CoRT thinking lessons). He paired it with deliberate questions: why does this exist, what is this boundary, whose frame is this. The "challenge the dominant idea" move and the boundary/continuity questions in the inventory descend from this line.

De Bono's challenge operation is also one of the routes into provocation (`Po`). This skill deliberately stops short of provocation. It borrows the interrogation of assumptions but keeps the output at the level of evidence and problem statements; provocative generation is left to lateral thinking.

**Chris Argyris and Donald Schön — double-loop learning.** *Theory in Practice* (1974) and *Organizational Learning* (1978) distinguish single-loop learning (fix the action) from double-loop learning (question the governing variables, norms, and assumptions that produced the action). Their distinction between espoused theory and theory-in-use is directly relevant: people often state one belief and act on another, and the behavioral evidence is the more reliable one. This is adjacent lineage, not an origin — they were describing organizational learning, not a facilitated assumption inventory. The emphasis on testing beliefs against observed practice, and on surfacing beliefs that are held tacitly, reflects this tradition.

**Peter Senge — mental models.** *The Fifth Discipline* (1990) popularized surfacing mental models as a discipline, drawing on Argyris's ladder of inference. The "inventory hidden beliefs" step is closer to Senge's popularization than to de Bono: the point is to make tacit models inspectable and open to revision. The ladder of inference is a useful diagnostic when an inventory entry is really a conclusion built on selected data.

**Assumption-based planning (RAND).** James Dewar, Carlisle Deal, and Joseph George, *Assumption-Based Planning* (1993), formalized identifying load-bearing assumptions, monitoring their validity, and taking shaping and hedging actions. The idea that assumptions differ in load-bearingness, and that some deserve monitoring rather than immediate testing, comes from this line. The impact × uncertainty ranking in the testing reference is a simplified product-discovery variant of the same idea.

**Assumption mapping (modern product practice).** David J. Bland and Alexander Osterwalder, *Testing Business Ideas* (2019), popularized plotting assumptions by importance and evidence, then designing cheap experiments. The probe-sizing tiers and the "evidence today" column are adapted from this practice. This is a practitioner tradition, not a research literature.

## What is local to this skill

- The four belief families (origin, source, boundary, continuity) are a teaching taxonomy assembled here for coverage. They overlap deliberately; they are lenses, not mutually exclusive categories.
- The three tests — falsify, unnecessary, reversed — are a synthesis. Falsification is standard scientific method; the "unnecessary" test resembles value engineering and de Bono's challenge of existence; the reversal test resembles inversion and assumption-negation techniques. Calling them "the three tests" is this skill's packaging.
- The five reframe patterns (negate, relax, invert, re-scope, re-attribute) are a local pattern language. They are not a validated instrument, and the names are descriptive rather than borrowed.
- The six-step process and capture table are designed for an AI-assisted conversation. They should be treated as scaffolding, not as an empirically validated protocol.

## What this skill is not

- It is not de Bono's lateral thinking. That method generates provocations and movement; this one inventories and tests beliefs. The overlap is the word "assumption."
- It is not first-principles thinking. First-principles rebuilds from verified fundamentals; this skill audits the current frame and reframes it. Use first-principles when the foundation itself is in doubt.
- It is not a decision procedure. It produces an assumption map, reframed statements, and probes. Choosing among options needs a separate method.
- It is not a substitute for research. An assumption is not evidence, and labeling something an assumption does not make testing it optional.

## Evidence note

There is no strong body of controlled evidence showing that facilitated assumption-challenging improves decision quality in organizations. What exists is indirect and mixed:

- Research on **fixation and design fixation** suggests that explicit prompts to reconsider initial framings can increase the variety of ideas, but effects depend on task and instruction quality.
- **Debiasing** research finds that warning people about bias is weak; structured procedures and concrete counter-cases tend to work better than exhortations. The inventory and decision-rule format are attempts to be concrete rather than exhortative.
- **Assumption-based planning** has practitioner case support, not randomized trials.
- **Product-discovery experiment practice** is supported mainly by field experience and case reports; survivorship in published case studies is substantial.

Treat the method as a structured way to slow down and externalize reasoning, not as a guarantee of better outcomes. The real quality comes from the evidence you gather and the honesty of the labels, not from the checklist.

## Attribution rules for use

- Name de Bono for the challenge operation and the boundary/continuity questions.
- Name Argyris and Schön for double-loop learning and espoused-versus-in-use theory.
- Name Dewar et al. for assumption-based planning and load-bearing assumptions.
- Name Bland and Osterwalder for assumption mapping and experiment sizing.
- Call this skill's taxonomy, patterns, and process adaptations. Do not attribute them to those authors.
- Do not claim that any of these methods has been validated as improving outcomes in general; describe what was observed in the specific case.

## Maintenance note

If the skill is revised, keep the distinction between borrowed and local content visible. Adding a pattern or test is cheap; misattributing it to a named tradition is not.
