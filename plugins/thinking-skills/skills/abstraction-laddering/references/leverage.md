# Choosing the Leverage Point

A ladder gives you many places to act. Leverage is about choosing one — plus the smallest supporting move above and below it — instead of treating every rung as equally actionable.

## Three axes

Score each candidate rung 1–3 on:

- **Impact** — how much of the outcome moves if this rung changes. 1 = cosmetic; 3 = would change the outcome for most of the affected population.
- **Controllability** — how much you can actually act on it with the authority and resources you have. 1 = someone else's decision; 3 = you can change it this quarter.
- **Evidence** — how well the rung is supported. 1 = speculation; 3 = observed or measured link.

Sum or plot them. The best leverage point is usually **impact 2–3, control 2–3, evidence ≥ 2**. A high-impact, low-control rung is a place to influence, not to act. A high-control, low-impact rung is busywork with good metrics.

## The four quadrants

| | Low control | High control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High impact** | **Advocacy** — build the case, find the owner, run a bounded pilot | **Act** — this is the leverage point |
| **Low impact** | **Ignore** — not your problem this cycle | **Automate or delegate** — do it cheaply or stop |

Move an advocacy rung into the act quadrant by shrinking scope (a pilot, one segment, a reversible change) or by borrowing authority. Do not confuse a convincing deck with a change in the system.

## Tall vs. short interventions

- **Tall intervention** — changes a purpose or function level (e.g., redefine success as "first useful result" instead of "account created"). High impact, slow, hard to reverse, needs alignment.
- **Short intervention** — changes a mechanism or action (e.g., defer one setup step). Fast, testable, reversible, but can be cosmetic if the link above is wrong.

The productive pattern is **short intervention in service of a tall hypothesis**: act at mechanism level, but state the function/purpose rung the result is supposed to move, and commit to a decision rule. This keeps tactics honest and strategy testable.

## Choosing among candidate rungs

1. **Eliminate wrong-level candidates.** If two rungs are at the same level, compare them on impact and evidence. If a rung is an orphan (no goal above), drop it.
2. **Find the binding constraint.** Which link in the ladder is most uncertain *and* most load-bearing? Intervening there produces the most learning per unit cost, even if it moves the outcome less.
3. **Prefer reversible.** When evidence is thin, choose the rung whose failure is cheap and recoverable.
4. **Check the counterfactual.** "If we did nothing here, would the outcome change anyway?" Trend, seasonality, and concurrent work can masquerade as leverage.
5. **Name the sacrifice.** Every choice spends capacity that another rung could have used. State what you are not doing.

## Leverage traps

- **High-altitude theater** — acting at vision level with slogans and no mechanism. Feels strategic; leaves the system unchanged.
- **Local optimization** — moving an action metric that does not propagate upward. Check the next rung before celebrating.
- **Proxy worship** — optimizing the measure of a function until it detaches from the purpose it served.
- **Single-rung thinking** — changing a mechanism while the goal above is wrong. The ladder exists to catch this.
- **Unfalsifiable choice** — picking a rung whose effect cannot be observed within the horizon. If you cannot see it move, you cannot learn.
- **Ownership confusion** — treating controllability as a reason to shrink ambition. Pair a controlled action with an advocacy move on the rung you do not own.

## Output

For the chosen point, write:

- The rung and its level.
- The intervention in one sentence.
- The rung above it that the intervention is supposed to move.
- The observable and the decision rule.
- The main assumption and its cheapest check.
- What you are deliberately not doing.

If the intervention succeeds, climb: which higher rung should be revisited next? If it fails, descend one rung or re-ladder the link. Leverage is a position to learn from, not a permanent home.
