# Question types

Six categories keep a storm from collapsing into one kind of question. The
categories are prompts, not a quota: a real storm will be uneven, and some
questions will fit two categories.

## 1. Clarifying — what do the words mean?

Use when the brief uses abstract nouns ("better," "faster," "engaged,"
"scale") or when two people could answer differently without noticing.

Stems:
- What exactly does ⟨term⟩ mean here, and what would count as an example?
- What is in scope, and what is explicitly out?
- What does success look like in observable terms, and who judges it?
- What is the unit, the denominator, or the baseline?
- What does "done" mean for this request?
- Which of these words would two stakeholders define differently?

High-yield when: the ask is one sentence; the team is arguing about solutions
but using the same words to mean different things.

Failure mode: definitional questions that never end. Cap them; if a definition
cannot be settled in one conversation, it is a stakeholder question instead.

## 2. Reframing — why this problem?

Use to locate the question inside the goal that produced it, and to test
whether the question is a means or an end.

Stems:
- What is the underlying goal, and why is it worth pursuing now?
- If this question were answered perfectly, what would change?
- Why this question rather than a neighboring one?
- What is the opposite question, and what would make it more useful?
- What problem would we have if this one disappeared?
- Who framed this question, and what did they leave out?
- What would make this question obsolete?

High-yield when: the brief is a solution ("add a dashboard"), a deadline is
the only reason for urgency, or the team cannot explain what decision the
answer enables.

Failure mode: infinite regress ("but why?"). Stop at the goal the requester
actually owns.

## 3. Stakeholder — who defines the problem?

Use when the brief speaks for users, customers, or "the business" without
naming them, and when costs and benefits fall on different people.

Stems:
- Who is affected by this, and who has been asked so far?
- Who decides, who pays, and who does the work?
- Whose definition of the problem currently wins, and why?
- Who benefits if the current situation continues?
- Who would be surprised to learn this is the question?
- Which user segment is assumed, and which is ignored?
- What would the least powerful affected person say the problem is?

High-yield when: the requester is not the person bearing the cost; adoption,
compliance, or internal politics are involved.

Failure mode: stakeholder theater — listing everyone without deciding whose
definition the work will serve.

## 4. Constraint — what is actually fixed?

Use when the space of answers has been pre-narrowed by budget, policy,
architecture, or a decision already announced.

Stems:
- What is fixed, and who has the authority to fix it?
- Which constraints are physics, which are policy, and which are habit?
- What would we do with twice the budget, or half the time?
- What if the deadline moved, or the launch were smaller?
- Which constraint was imposed without a recorded reason?
- What is the cost of satisfying this constraint, and who bears it?
- What would have to be true for the constraint to disappear?

High-yield when: every option looks impossible, or the first answer is "we
can't because ⟨system/policy⟩."

Failure mode: relitigating real constraints. Verify authority and evidence
before treating a fixed constraint as negotiable.

## 5. Causal — what produces this, and how would we know?

Use when the brief states a symptom and jumps to a remedy, or when the
evidence is a single anecdote.

Stems:
- What is the evidence that this is a problem, and how was it collected?
- What mechanism would produce this outcome?
- When did it start, and what changed around then?
- Where does it happen most, and where does it not happen at all?
- What would we expect to see if the proposed cause were true?
- What else could produce the same observation?
- How would we detect the problem if nobody had reported it?
- What is the counterfactual — what happens without intervention?

High-yield when: a solution is already chosen, the problem is new, or the
data is a dashboard aggregate with no denominator.

Failure mode: plausible stories treated as causes. Label speculation and name
the evidence that would distinguish mechanisms.

## 6. Generative — what if, and how might we?

Use after the problem is framed well enough to explore, to open directions
without committing to one.

Stems:
- What if the constraint did not exist?
- How might we achieve the goal with no new tooling?
- What would make this easy rather than merely possible?
- What is the simplest version that would teach us something?
- Who has solved an analogous problem, and what did they assume?
- What if we did nothing — what would happen?
- What would a hostile or lazy user do?
- What would we try if we could not measure the result?

High-yield when: the team is stuck between two incremental options, or the
revised statement still points at a single solution.

Failure mode: generating solutions under the label of questions. Keep the
grammar interrogative and defer evaluation.

## Coverage check

Before clustering, scan the storm for gaps:

- At least three clarifying questions about the brief's key nouns.
- At least two questions about who owns and benefits from the frame.
- At least one question that challenges a stated constraint.
- At least one causal question that asks for evidence.
- At least one question whose answer could change the problem statement.

If a category is empty, ask why rather than mechanically filling it.
