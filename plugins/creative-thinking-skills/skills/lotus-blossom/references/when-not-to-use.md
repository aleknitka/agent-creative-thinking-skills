# When NOT to Use Lotus Blossom — and What to Use Instead

Lotus Blossom is radial expansion from one theme. If the job is to decide, to map independent dimensions, or to find the question, another method fits better. The 72-slot board is not a default; it is a choice about geometry.

## Quick triage

| Your situation | Better fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Need to choose among produced ideas | `options-portfolio`, dot voting, decision analysis | Another expansion pass multiplies the pile instead of reducing it |
| Several independent design choices; need combinations or coverage | `morphological-analysis` | The box enumerates dimensions and prunes incompatible combinations; the lotus does not model independence |
| Need eight comparable concepts in a short burst | `crazy-eights` | 8 slots in 8 minutes; 72 slots is the wrong scale and invites elaboration |
| No central theme yet, or the question is wrong | `question-storming`, `lateral-thinking`, `divergent-thinking` | Expand a bad center and you get 72 confident wrong answers |
| One baseline to improve incrementally | `scamper` | Seven targeted operators on an existing object, not radial generation from a theme |
| Need to stress-test a chosen concept | `premortem` | Failure imagination, not breadth |
| Need to plan across external futures | `scenario-planning` | Uncertainty about the world, not internal idea volume |
| Missing facts about users, constraints, or feasibility | **Research first**, then return | A 72-slot board on unknown ground produces elaborate fiction |
| Need to converge under a deadline | Plain decision meeting with criteria | The board's value is coverage; when time is short, coverage is a liability |

## Lotus Blossom vs morphological analysis

Both are structured and both produce many combinations; they answer different questions.

- **Morphological analysis** asks *what combinations exist*. It decomposes the solution into independent dimensions, lists options per dimension, prunes incompatibilities, and samples or scores the surviving space. Its strength is coverage and a defensible shortlist. Use it when the design space has visible independent choices (trigger × channel × timing × owner) and you need to know what you have not considered.
- **Lotus Blossom** asks *what ideas live under this theme*. It partitions the theme into angles and generates concrete ideas per angle. Its strength is traceability and sub-theme visibility. Use it when one theme needs depth and you want to see which angle is thin.
- If the dimensions are causally entangled, neither tool is first: model the system (`first-principles`, `causal-loop-diagram`) before mapping options.

A practical chain: Lotus Blossom on the theme to surface candidate angles → morphological analysis on the two or three angles that look like independent dimensions → sample combinations → probe. Do not present a lotus board as a coverage argument; coverage is the box's claim, not the blossom's.

## Lotus Blossom vs Crazy Eights

Crazy Eights is a breadth sprint; Lotus Blossom is a breadth structure.

- **Crazy Eights** is better when you need eight comparable concepts quickly, the prompt is sharp, and comparison is the point. It samples the space by intuition and is deliberately shallow.
- **Lotus Blossom** is better when eight concepts are not enough, when you want a second layer under each direction, or when the group needs to see sub-themes before details. It is slower (25–40 minutes) and produces more material than most sessions can use.
- Crazy Eights' weakness — over-sampling familiar mechanisms — is partly corrected by Lotus Blossom's petal structure, but the lotus has its own weakness: 72 slots invite quantity theatre. If nobody will prune, use Crazy Eights.
- Eight slots in Crazy Eights are a comparison set. Eight petals in Lotus Blossom are a partition of a theme; they are not alternatives to choose between.

A practical chain: Crazy Eights to find candidate directions → Lotus Blossom on the most promising direction → prune to a shortlist → probe. Or the reverse: lotus board first, then Crazy Eights on each of the two best petals when the theme is broad.

## Other adjacent methods

- **Brainwriting** removes social blocking and produces many distinct ideas; use it when the group dynamic, not the theme structure, is the problem. It does not organise ideas by angle.
- **SCAMPER** applies seven operators to an existing baseline; use it when you have something to improve rather than a theme to expand. Its operations overlap with the lotus's expansion operations, but SCAMPER stays on one object.
- **Forced connections / concept blending** create novelty by joining unrelated material. Use them when the lotus's inside-the-theme ideas are too polite and you need outside material.
- **Ideal final result** works backwards from a perfect outcome; use it when constraints are the thing to suspend before expanding.
- **Futures wheel** is also radial but expands *consequences* of one change, not ideas under a theme. If the question is "what follows if X happens?", use the futures wheel.

## Overkill signals

Lotus Blossom is the wrong tool when:

- The candidate set is already large and the work is selection. Adding 72 slots makes the decision harder, not better.
- The theme is a slogan ("make it delightful", "improve UX"). Narrow it to an audience and a moment first.
- The team will not prune. An unpruned board is a wall of text; the method's value is in the second half.
- The output will be judged as a recommendation. Seventy-two slots are material, not a plan.
- The problem is a single technical contradiction with a known trade-off. Resolve the contradiction, then expand around it.
- The session has under 15 minutes. Use Crazy Eights or a plain list.
- The work needs one compliant, defensible answer. Breadth can obscure that one constraint decides the issue.

## Handoff triggers

| Next | Trigger |
|---|---|
| `morphological-analysis` | Two or three petals behave like independent dimensions; map them explicitly |
| `crazy-eights` | One petal is the clear direction and needs eight quick comparable concepts before piloting |
| `scamper` | One promising slot needs systematic variation on an existing baseline |
| `concept-blending` | Two slots from different petals should be fused into one stronger concept |
| `options-portfolio` | More survivors than can be probed; need portfolio-level selection |
| `premortem` | A concept is chosen and needs failure analysis before commitment |
| `question-storming` | Petals keep collapsing into synonyms; the theme is underspecified |
| `first-principles` | Every petal reproduces the same mechanism; the model of the problem is wrong |
| **Research** | Feasibility, user behaviour, or constraints are simply unknown |

## Anti-patterns

- **Petal synonyms** — eight names for one angle; the board is a single block photocopied.
- **Three-and-done** — filling three slots per petal and calling it a board; the method's depth claim is unearned.
- **Quantity theatre** — 72 slots generated, none pruned, nobody probes; the board becomes a deliverable instead of an input.
- **Premature scoring** — ranking slots while filling petals, which shrinks the board to existing preferences.
- **Single-winner harvest** — one idea extracted from 72 without a probe; the structure produced options, not evidence.
- **Center drift** — the theme changes between petals, so the blocks no longer answer the same question.
- **Skipping the petal check** — overlapping petals produce duplicate ideas and a false sense of breadth.
