# Facilitation — Independent Generation and Anti-Anchoring

The premortem's value comes from surfacing doubts that people would not say aloud in the planning meeting. Public risk brainstorming reliably produces polite, already-mitigated risks and anchors on whoever speaks first — usually the most senior or most optimistic person. The protocol below protects the independent signal.

## Preparation (10–15 min)

- **Freeze the proposal.** A 3–5 sentence summary: what ships, for whom, by when, who commits resources, the success measure, and hard constraints.
- **Set a failure horizon.** A concrete date or milestone ("six months after launch") at which the project has failed. Vague horizons produce vague reasons.
- **Choose participants (4–8).** Include front-line people, adjacent teams, and at least one habitual skeptic. Homogeneous optimists produce a homogeneous list.
- **Do not circulate the existing risk register or a draft list of concerns beforehand.** It anchors the room on the risks you already know.
- **State the rules up front:** the failure is a premise, reasons are not disloyalty, no names are attached, and the output will change the plan.

## Protocol

1. **Brief the plan without advocacy.** Facts and constraints, not a pitch. The plan owner states what is proposed and what is still undecided — then stops selling.
2. **Declare the failure.** "It is ⟨date⟩. The project failed." The leader states it as a fact and does not invite debate about whether it will fail.
3. **Silent generation (5–10 min).** Each person writes 8–12 reasons alone. No talking, no shared document, no peeking. This is the anti-anchoring core: independent samples before any social influence.
4. **Round-robin capture.** Each person reads one reason per turn; record it verbatim. No critique, no rebuttal, no explanation until every reason is on the board. Rotating turns keep one voice from dominating.
5. **Clarify, do not debate.** Ask questions for understanding ("what would that look like?"), never for defence ("why do you think that?"). The person who raised a reason is not required to defend it.
6. **Cluster and score together.** Group by causal mechanism. Have each participant score independently first, then share scores; discuss only the outliers and disagreements.
7. **Harden and assign.** Convert the top reasons into prevention and monitoring actions with owners and dates. End by reading back what changed in the plan.

## Anti-anchoring rules

- **Write first, talk second.** Even five minutes of silent writing neutralises most of the seniority effect.
- **Leader speaks last** during capture, or not at all after declaring the failure. A sponsor's opening opinion sets the ceiling for candour.
- **Randomise the speaking order** after the first pass, or collect cards and read them anonymously.
- **Prime with categories, not conclusions.** "Consider incentives" is fine; "I'm worried CS won't adopt it" is an anchor.
- **Separate the plan from its author.** Critique of the design must not require disloyalty to the person who proposed it.
- **Keep two lists apart:** "why it failed" (generation) and "what we will change" (hardening). Mixing them turns every reason into a defended mitigation.
- **Watch the plan owner's reaction.** Visible defensiveness during generation teaches the room to self-censor; route the first clarification questions to neutral participants.

## Solo mode

1. Write 10–12 reasons in one timed pass without editing.
2. Force the categories you skipped — most solos overproduce external and underproduce incentive or coordination reasons.
3. Run a second pass as "the person who would be most affected but least consulted". Write their objections in their voice.
4. Score only after both passes; do not let early scores truncate generation.

## AI-assisted mode

- Have the human write reasons first when anchoring is a risk; then ask the model for reasons in categories the human missed.
- The model must generate, not reassure or evaluate: no "this is a strong plan, but…".
- Treat model reasons as **hypotheses**, label them as such, and verify claims about your organisation or market before acting.
- Use the model to cluster and draft the scoring table, but keep likelihood and impact judgments human-owned.

## Timing

| Setting | Total | Silent generation | Capture + cluster | Score + harden |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solo / small | 45–60 min | 8 min | 15 min | 25 min |
| Standard team | 90 min | 10 min | 30 min | 45 min |
| High-stakes | 2 × 90 min | 10 min | 40 min | 70 min + follow-up |

## Common facilitation failures

- Skipping silent generation because "we're a candid team" — candour in the room is not the same as independence.
- Letting the plan owner rebut each reason in turn; the exercise becomes a defence and the real doubts stay private.
- Collecting generic risks instead of mechanisms; the hardening step then has nothing concrete to act on.
- Ending with a list and no owners; a premortem that changes no decision is theatre.
- Running it after commitment has been announced, when speaking up carries career risk and the plan can no longer change.
