# Provenance — Lineage, Attribution, and Evidence Limits

## The lineage

- **Edward de Bono — random entry.** In *Lateral Thinking: Creativity Step by Step* (1970), *The Mechanism of Mind* (1969), and *Serious Creativity* (1992), de Bono argues that self-organizing memory forms asymmetric patterns, so deliberate **random entry** can open a path that the dominant pattern excludes. His exercises include opening a dictionary at random, taking a word from an unrelated field, and using it as a starting point rather than a subject. He generally calls this **random entry** or **random stimulus**, not "forced connections."
- **William J. J. Gordon — Synectics.** *Synectics: The Development of Creativity* (1961), developed with George M. Prince at Arthur D. Little, formalized making the familiar strange through analogy. The four Synectics analogies used here — direct, personal, symbolic, fantasy — come from this tradition. Synectics groups also used deliberately unrelated material as a springboard; the term **forced connections** appears in the broader creative-problem-solving and brainstorming literature for making such a link deliberately, without a single canonical protocol.
- **Alex Osborn and brainstorming.** *Applied Imagination* (1953) codified deferred judgment and quantity-before-quality, the generation stance this method inherits. Osborn's own "idea spurring" used checklists rather than random stimuli, so it is context rather than a direct ancestor of the draw step.
- **Joy Paul Guilford.** His 1950 APA address and later work distinguished **originality** from **appropriateness** in creative production. The novelty × usefulness evaluation here is a practical descendant of that distinction, not a measurement instrument.
- **The standard definition.** Runco and Jaeger, "The standard definition of creativity" (*Creativity Research Journal*, 2012), summarizes creativity as requiring both originality and effectiveness. It supports the two-axis evaluation, not any particular scoring scale.

## What is original here

The following are workflow adaptations for this repository, not claims about the sources:

- The six-step process (intake, stimulus type, generate, force, extract, probe).
- The four-type stimulus taxonomy (word, object, image, concept) and its selection criteria.
- The property-inventory categories, property-mapping table, and analogy sprint instructions.
- The novelty and usefulness anchors, decision rules, kill criteria, and value-statement format.
- The three worked examples, comparison tables, and handoff map.
- The offline `scripts/connect.py` banks and prompt output.

Attribute the core moves — random entry and deliberate analogy — to de Bono, Gordon, and Prince. Attribute the elaborations above to this skill as adaptations.

## Evidence and limits

- There is **no controlled trial of this six-step protocol**. Its value rests on the pattern-breaking argument and on practitioner experience, not on an effect size.
- Meta-analytic reviews of creativity training (for example, Scott, Leritz, and Mumford, "The effectiveness of creativity training: A quantitative review," *Creativity Research Journal*, 2004) report positive but **heterogeneous** effects across many training designs; estimates vary with the design and the outcome measured. They do not validate random stimulation specifically, and the variation across studies is large. Do not quote an effect size for this method.
- A plausible mechanism is **variability–fit trade-off**: an unrelated stimulus tends to increase originality while making appropriateness harder to achieve. Treat that as an inference, not a measured law. The evaluation step exists precisely because the trade-off is real in practice.
- The method is qualitative. It surfaces candidate directions and testable mechanisms; it does not estimate probabilities, discover unknown unknowns, or replace domain research, pilots, or user evidence.
- Known biases: novelty halo, ownership bias, facilitator curation, and strained-link politeness. The randomness discipline and the kill criteria are mitigations, not guarantees.
- A forced connection is a **hypothesis**. Mark observed, inferred, and speculative claims, and do not cite this skill's examples as evidence about your own context.
