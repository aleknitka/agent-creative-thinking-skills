# Stimuli — Types, Selection, and Randomness

A forced connection is only as good as its distance. A stimulus that is already relevant produces the same ideas with a new mascot. This note covers what to draw, where to draw it from, and how to keep the draw honest.

## What makes a good stimulus

- **Concrete.** A thing with properties you can list, not an abstraction ("synergy", "innovation").
- **Unrelated.** No causal, structural, or vocabulary link to the problem domain.
- **Property-rich.** At least 8 literal properties: affordances, failure modes, lifecycle, relationships.
- **Neutral.** No charged political, religious, or personal content; no person's name. Shock is not distance.
- **Not already in the room.** Reject anything the team already uses as a metaphor or has recently discussed.

Rejection test: *Can you state in one sentence why this stimulus is relevant?* If yes, it is too close. Redraw.

## The four types

| Type | What it supplies | Cost | Best when | Failure risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Word** | A compact label; properties must be supplied by you | Lowest | You need many draws quickly, or group energy | Abstraction or cliché; the word suggests a pun |
| **Object** | Affordances, materials, wear, one-way mechanisms | Low (pick up something) | You want physical constraints and verbs | You describe the object's purpose instead of its properties |
| **Image** | Relations and absences the vocabulary does not cover | Medium (find a captionless image) | Words keep returning the same associations | You interpret the image as a symbol before describing it |
| **Concept** | A developed mechanism from another field | Highest (needs accurate knowledge) | Word links are exhausted and you want machinery | It becomes analogical search or a half-understood buzzword |

## Word

Draw from an offline bank, a random dictionary page, a field-guide index, or the helper script. Prefer **concrete nouns** with a physical referent: tools, natural objects, buildings, instruments, containers. Avoid abstract nouns, proper nouns, and words that already belong to your industry.

The word is only a handle. If you cannot list literal properties from knowledge, the draw is wasted; replace it rather than inventing facts about it.

A short bank ships with `scripts/connect.py` (original, MIT-safe). Expand it only with public-domain or compatible sources, and keep it concrete.

## Object

Pick a physical object you can inspect: a turnstile, a tea strainer, a padlock, a seed packet, a fire escape, a library date stamp.

Describe before interpreting:

1. **Material and parts** — what it is made of, what the pieces are.
2. **Affordances** — what it lets you do, what it forbids, what it makes effortful.
3. **Dynamics** — what moves, what ratchets, what resets, what wears out.
4. **Relations** — who operates it, who waits, what it separates, what it counts.
5. **Failure** — how it breaks, jams, or is defeated.

Photograph or sketch the object if it is shared; the properties, not the object, are the artifact.

## Image

Use a captionless photograph or illustration with no obvious business content — a tide pool, a bridge repaint, a night-market stall, a beehive cross-section. Remove the caption: captions supply the interpretation you are trying to avoid.

Describe in three columns — **things / relations / absences**: what is present, what connects to what, and what is conspicuously missing (a path, a label, a person, a boundary). Absences often produce the least obvious properties.

Then circle two details you did not notice on the first pass and use those. Do not ask "what does this image symbolize?" — ask what is literally happening.

## Concept

A named concept from an unrelated field can supply a mechanism that words and objects do not: escrow, succession, quorum, load balancing, keystone species, seed dormancy, dead reckoning, airlock, contour lines.

Two disciplines:

- **Draw by arbitrary entry, not by fit.** Choose from an index, glossary, or syllabus before you know what it will give you. Searching for the field that is "structurally similar" is `analogical-thinking`, not this method.
- **Know the concept well enough to state its actual mechanism.** If you cannot explain it without the source text in front of you, pick another. A half-understood concept produces decoration, not a mechanism.

Do not use the concept's jargon in the output until the connection has survived evaluation. Jargon makes a strained link sound sophisticated.

## Randomness discipline

- **Generate before judging.** Draw 3–5 candidates, then pick one. Do not redraw because the first stimulus seems unhelpful — difficulty is often where the new link is.
- **Blind selection.** If working with a group, number the candidates and have someone else pick. The facilitator should not choose the stimulus that fits their hypothesis.
- **One redraw is allowed, with a reason.** "No literal properties I can state" and "obvious domain link" are valid reasons. "I don't like it" is not.
- **Record the draw.** Stimulus, type, source, and who chose it. The record makes the method auditable and stops post-hoc curation.
- **Do not reuse a stimulus across problems in the same session.** The second use carries the first problem's associations.

## Recording template

```
Problem:        <one sentence>
Stimulus:       <verbatim>
Type:           word / object / image / concept
Source:         <random page, desk object, script seed, index entry>
Why unrelated:  <one sentence: the domain it comes from, not why it fits>
Candidates:     <the other draws, so curation is visible>
```

If you cannot fill "why unrelated" without describing a fit, the draw failed.
