# Provenance — Attribution, Origin, and Evidence

## Named method and lineage

- **Alex F. Osborn** — inventor of brainstorming — published a checklist of **73 questions** to stimulate creative thinking in *Applied Imagination* (1953), including "What can be substituted?", "What can be combined?", "What can be reversed?". The list was unstructured and hard to remember.
- **Bob Eberle** — American educator — compressed Osborn's 73 questions into **seven operators** and wrapped them in the **SCAMPER** acronym in *SCAMPER: Games for Imagination Development* (1971), aiming to make the checklist accessible for classroom and adult education. The simplification succeeded so well that SCAMPER far surpassed Osborn's list in recognition.

The seven operators — **Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to other use, Eliminate, Reverse** — are Eberle's framing of Osborn's checklist. The trigger questions and service-specific examples in this skill are original facilitation guidance, not Eberle's text.

## Adaptations in this skill

- The preparation and subject-concreteness checklist (3–5 sentence baseline), the three execution modes (sequential / selective / rotation), the follow-up sequence (cluster → evaluate → select → next steps), the banking onboarding worked example, and the comparison table (SCAMPER vs. brainstorming / brainwriting / morphological box) are **practical workflow adaptations** for this repository.
- The guidance to prioritize Eliminate and Substitute for legacy processes (highest ROI where steps exist for historical reasons) reflects field experience synthesized in the SI Labs guide cited in the research notes — presented here as heuristic, not as experimental finding.

## Evidence and limits

- Controlled effectiveness studies for SCAMPER are **rare**; the method rests primarily on theoretical plausibility and field experience rather than experimental evidence. The SI Labs guide notes this explicitly.
- The method is strongest for **incremental improvement** of existing services and products — the most frequent innovation type in business — and is a weaker fit for radical new-concept development, where divergent or combinatorial methods are more appropriate.
- No claim about SCAMPER's superiority over other methods is made as an empirical finding; tradeoffs are presented as facilitation heuristics to be validated in context.
