# Provenance, Lineage, and Evidence Limits

## Origin

Crazy Eights (also written "Crazy 8s") is a divergent ideation exercise popularized by **Jake Knapp** at **Google Ventures (GV)** and documented in the Design Sprint literature, most prominently the book **Sprint** (2016) by Jake Knapp, John Zeratsky, and Braden Kowitz. The canonical physical form: fold a sheet of paper into eight frames and sketch eight different ideas in eight minutes — roughly one minute per frame.

GV's 2012 "Product Design Sprint" write-up on the diverge day describes the same exercise; facilitator instructions across GV-derived kits vary in timing (strict one-minute sketches, or shorter rounds such as 40 seconds per frame while keeping eight frames). Treat "8 ideas / 8 frames" as the stable core and the exact seconds as a facilitation choice. When running the method, state your timing up front rather than letting participants discover it.

The exercise sits in a longer Design Sprint sequence: understand → diverge (Crazy Eights among other ideation moves) → decide → prototype → test. This skill extracts the divergence step as a standalone method. It does not teach the full sprint.

## What is original here

- **The text adaptation.** Crazy Eights is a sketching method. This skill replaces sketches with one-to-two-sentence concepts plus a short mechanism label. That substitution is ours. It trades the speed and ambiguity of drawing for verbal precision, and it loses the visual, non-linear thinking that sketching affords. The mechanism and "differs from" fields are the compensation: they force each slot to be a different idea rather than a differently worded one.
- **The slot template and helper.** The eight-slot worksheet and the offline helper script are original scaffolding, not reproductions of GV materials.
- **The variety audit and clustering guidance.** Assembled for this skill from general ideation practice (defer judgment, separate generation from selection, cluster by mechanism). Not proprietary.

No GV slides, templates, or book text are reproduced. The method name and the high-level procedure are attributed, not licensed.

## Lineage and neighbours

- **Brainstorming (Osborn, 1953):** defer judgment, seek quantity, build on ideas. Crazy Eights is a timeboxed, individual-first application of these rules.
- **Nominal group and brainwriting techniques:** silent parallel generation followed by sharing. Crazy Eights' silence during generation and gallery share follow the same logic: avoid anchoring and production blocking.
- **Design charrettes and "eight-up" sketching:** architecture and design practice has long used rapid multiple-sketch rounds. Knapp's version made the eight-frame constraint a fixed ritual.
- **Design Sprint:** the parent process; see `Sprint` and GV's sprint resources for the full sequence and for how Crazy Eights feeds the "decide" step.

## Evidence limits

This is a **practitioner heuristic, not a validated intervention**. Relevant cautions:

- Research on brainstorming consistently finds that groups produce fewer and less original ideas than the same number of people working alone (production blocking, evaluation apprehension, social loafing). Crazy Eights' silent generation and individual slots respond to this, but the method itself has not been tested as a package in controlled studies.
- Time pressure reliably increases output rate; its effect on **quality** is mixed and task-dependent. The method deliberately trades depth for breadth. Do not claim that eight fast concepts are better than three considered ones — claim that they make the space visible sooner.
- The "eight" is arbitrary. It is small enough to finish and large enough to break the first-idea anchor. There is no evidence that eight is optimal.
- Sketching carries information that text does not (spatial arrangement, emphasis, ambiguity that invites interpretation). The text adaptation loses this. If the team can sketch, sketch; use this version when the medium is text or when participants are remote and non-designers.
- Expect a positivity bias: teams tend to produce feasible, on-brand concepts. The forced variety seeds and the last-slot rules exist to counteract it, not because they are proven.

## Attribution in use

When presenting results, say: "Crazy Eights, popularized by Jake Knapp/Google Ventures, adapted here to text." Do not present the adaptation as the original method, and do not imply endorsement by GV or the Sprint authors. If you publish a derivative template, attribute the method and note your changes.
