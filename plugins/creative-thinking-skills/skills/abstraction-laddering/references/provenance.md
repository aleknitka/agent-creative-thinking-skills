# Provenance and Evidence Limits

## The technique

"Abstraction laddering" is a design-thinking method for reconsidering a problem statement by broadening or narrowing its focus. LUMA Institute documents it as a problem-framing method: write the initial statement on a middle rung, move **up** by asking *Why?* to expand scope, move **down** by asking *How?* to make the challenge more concrete, then choose a statement to take into ideation. The name, the worksheet, and the why/how pairing come from that practice ([LUMA Institute, "Abstraction Laddering"](https://www.luma-institute.com/abstraction-laddering/)).

This skill keeps the core move and adds material that is explicitly **an adaptation**:

- a five-level vocabulary (vision / purpose / function / mechanism / action) and coherence checks between adjacent rungs;
- scoring for leverage (impact × controllability × evidence);
- a probe step with pre-committed decision rules;
- explicit handoffs to assumption-challenging, ideation, and causal diagnosis.

None of the additions are part of the original method. Call them adaptations.

## Related lineages (distinct, often conflated)

**Means-end chain and laddering interviews.** In consumer research, "laddering" is a depth-interview technique that elicits chains from product attributes to consequences to personal values (Gutman, "A Means-End Chain Model Based on Consumer Categorization Processes," *Journal of Marketing*, 1982; Reynolds & Olson, *Understanding Consumer Decision Making: The Means-End Approach to Marketing and Advertising Strategy*, 2001; Reynolds & Gutman, "Laddering Theory, Method, Analysis, and Interpretation," *Journal of Advertising Research*, 1988). Same up-the-ladder logic, different job: eliciting a respondent's value structure, not reframing a design problem. Treat it as a sibling, not the source of this method.

**Bateson's logical levels.** Gregory Bateson (*Steps to an Ecology of Mind*, 1972) described levels of learning and abstraction, where each level conditions the level below. This is a theoretical ancestor of "levels" thinking, not a problem-framing procedure. The popular "logical levels of change" model (environment → behavior → capability → belief → identity → mission), associated with NLP and Robert Dilts, is a later synthesis; this skill does not rely on it and does not treat its level names as validated.

**Five whys.** Associated with Sakichi Toyoda and the Toyota Production System (Taiichi Ohno, *Toyota Production System*, 1988), five whys descends causally toward a root cause of a defect. It shares the iterative "why" but answers a different question: cause, not purpose. Do not present five whys as the origin of abstraction laddering.

**Means-end analysis.** Newell and Simon's means-end analysis (*Human Problem Solving*, 1972) is a search heuristic that reduces the difference between current and goal state. It shares the means/ends vocabulary and nothing else operationally.

**Question-storming and reframing methods.** Various design and facilitation traditions generate alternative problem statements. They overlap in spirit; none is the direct source of the ladder worksheet.

## Evidence limits

- **No controlled evidence** establishes that abstraction laddering produces better outcomes than ordinary problem framing. The documented support is practitioner experience and its use in design curricula. Treat it as a structured prompt, not a validated intervention.
- **The levels are a vocabulary, not an ontology.** The five labels are useful for spotting mixed statements and missing rungs; they are not a claim about how cognition or organizations are structured.
- **Ladders rationalize as easily as they reveal.** A fluent chain of whys can make a preferred solution look inevitable. The coherence checks and evidence labels exist to counter this; they do not eliminate it.
- **Stopping points are judgment calls.** "Useful ceiling" and "observable floor" depend on scope, budget, and authority. Record the stop reason so later readers can disagree with it.
- **Provenance claims here are best-effort.** Citations name the works and ideas as commonly attributed; page-level claims are not reproduced. When precision matters, consult the originals.

## Attribution in artifacts

When using this skill in a deliverable, say what it is: a problem-framing ladder adapted from design practice, with added levels, coherence checks, and decision rules. Do not attribute the adaptations to LUMA, Gutman, or Bateson.
