# The Six Structures — Purpose, Timing, Steps, Failure Signals

Each structure is a container: a question, a grouping, a timebox, and an output. Run the container as written before adapting it. Times are for a group of 8–30; scale the all-group share, not the silent and pair rounds.

## 1-2-4-All

**Purpose.** Engage everyone simultaneously in generating questions, ideas, and suggestions, then converge through progressively larger groups. The scaling is the point: the silent minute protects independent thought, pairs reduce the cost of speaking, quads do the first synthesis, and the whole group only sees material that survived two filters.

**Time.** 12 minutes minimum for a small group; 20 minutes with 30+ people and a proper all-group harvest. Ratio: 1 minute silent, 2 minutes pairs, 4 minutes quads, 5 minutes whole group.

**Steps.**
1. State one answerable question and the output format ("three bullet points per quad"). Start the silent minute.
2. Everyone writes alone, no talking. Fragments and questions are acceptable.
3. Pairs exchange what they wrote; each person restates the other's point before adding their own.
4. Pairs join into quads; each pair contributes its strongest one or two items, then the quad clusters and names them.
5. Quads post or report only what is new, contested, or missing. Harvest on one board; read dissent aloud.

**Failure signals.** The silent minute is used to check phones or starts as whispering; pairs self-select into the two most senior people; quads read their full lists; the facilitator adds their own opinion during the harvest. Any of these restores the hierarchy the structure exists to flatten.

## Impromptu Networking

**Purpose.** Build connection fast and surface the challenges people actually carry, before any agenda item. It is relationship-first divergence: the content is what people say when asked "what challenge are you working on?" three times with three different partners.

**Time.** 15–25 minutes. Three rounds of roughly 4 minutes (2 minutes per person), plus a short setup and a 5-minute pattern harvest.

**Steps.**
1. Pose one question — usually "What challenge are you working on right now?" or "What are you trying to accomplish?" People stand and find a partner they do not know well.
2. Two minutes each, uninterrupted. One speaks, one listens; the listener may ask one clarifying question at the end.
3. Signal the switch; second person speaks for two minutes.
4. New partner, repeat. Three rounds maximum; novelty drops sharply after three.
5. Harvest: each person names one pattern, resource, or overlap they heard. Capture as a pattern list, not minutes.

**Failure signals.** People stay with the first partner; the round becomes a pitch or a status update; the harvest is skipped, so the networking leaves no usable artifact; the group is too large and the room too loud to hear a partner.

## 15% Solutions

**Purpose.** Shift from "the system must change" to "what can I do without more authority, budget, or headcount?" Fifteen percent is the discretionary space each person already controls. The output is action that can start this week.

**Time.** 20–30 minutes: 5 minutes writing, 10 minutes in threes, 10 minutes whole-group harvest.

**Steps.**
1. State the challenge and the constraint clearly: no new resources, no new mandate, no waiting for someone else.
2. Each person writes their own 15%: what they can do, starting now, with what they already have. One action per line, concrete enough to start.
3. In threes, each person describes their list in two minutes. The others ask only "what would make that easier?" and "what is the first 30 minutes?" — no advice on the whole system.
4. Sharpen each action until it has a first step, an owner, and a date.
5. Whole group: collect actions on a board, cluster by theme, and note where several people named the same blocker. The shared blocker becomes a separate escalation item, not a reason to wait.

**Failure signals.** Lists say "get leadership to…" or "hire…"; the threes turn into complaint sessions; actions are aspirations ("improve onboarding") rather than a first move; nobody writes a date.

## TRIZ (Wreck-the-Org)

**Purpose.** Make counterproductive practices visible by designing failure deliberately, then asking which of those designs the organisation is already running. It is a facilitation-forward inversion: the group names its own dysfunction without anyone having to accuse anyone.

**Time.** 45–60 minutes: 5 minutes framing, 10 minutes generating worst-result actions, 5 minutes voting, 10 minutes comparing with reality, 15 minutes building the stop list.

**Steps.**
1. State the goal positively ("customers onboard smoothly within a week").
2. Ask: "What would we do to guarantee the worst possible result?" Generate a long, specific list. Encourage precision — "make every new customer talk to a different person" beats "be bad".
3. Vote for the five to seven actions that would most reliably produce failure.
4. Ask the turn: "Which of these are we already doing, at least partially?" Mark them honestly. No defending, no explaining yet.
5. Build a stop list: what will we stop, who owns stopping it, and what will we do instead? The replacement behaviour is part of the commitment, not an afterthought.
6. Schedule the first review against the stop list, not against intentions.

**Failure signals.** The worst-result ideas are jokes or caricatures, which protects the group from recognition; step 4 is skipped because it feels accusatory; the stop list contains "communicate better" rather than a named practice; no one is assigned to stop anything; a senior person vetoes items during generation.

## Wicked Questions

**Purpose.** Put a paradox into words: two commitments that are both true and that pull against each other. A good wicked question cannot be answered by choosing a side; it changes the conversation from resolving the tension to managing it deliberately.

**Time.** 25–40 minutes: 5 minutes individual writing, 5 minutes pairs, 10 minutes crafting questions, 10 minutes testing and selecting, 5 minutes harvest.

**Steps.**
1. Ask people to write down tensions they experience as "we need X, but we also need Y", where both sides are real commitments.
2. In pairs, combine and sharpen the tensions; drop any where one side is only a preference.
3. Craft the question in the form "How can we … and … simultaneously?" Both clauses must name something the group genuinely values.
4. Test each candidate: Is either side false? Can it be answered by choosing one side? Does it describe daily experience? Does it open options rather than force a trade-off?
5. Select two or three questions that survive. Post them where the team can see them; use them as standing frames for later decisions.

**Failure signals.** One side is a strawman ("how can we move fast and also follow every process?"), producing a fake paradox; the group starts solving instead of holding; questions are abstract enough to fit any organisation; the selected questions are never used again.

## Ecocycle Planning

**Purpose.** See a portfolio of activities as a living system with four phases — gestation/birth, maturity, creative destruction, and renewal — plus two traps. It makes renewal and stopping visible, which linear portfolio reviews hide.

**Time.** 60–120 minutes. Abbreviated version: 45 minutes for a small portfolio, with the map drawn in advance.

**Steps.**
1. Draw a figure-eight on a large surface. Label the four arcs: gestation and birth (new, fragile, few results), maturity (productive, scaled, efficient), creative destruction (deliberately ending or releasing), renewal (new connections, experiments, learning).
2. Mark the two traps: the **poverty trap** (stuck in gestation, never resourced to mature) and the **rigidity trap** (stuck in maturity, protecting what works).
3. Place every activity, project, or commitment on the map. Use one note per item, sized by effort and coloured by owner.
4. Diagnose: which items are starved in gestation? Which are held in maturity past their value? What is missing entirely from renewal?
5. Select one or two items for creative destruction — not because they failed, but because they now consume capacity that renewal needs. Name what will be released and how.
6. Select one or two renewal seeds and the smallest experiment that would justify moving them toward birth. Assign owners and a review date.

**Failure signals.** Everything lands in maturity; destruction candidates are only "bad" projects, so the group avoids ending good-but-finished work; the map becomes a status report with no lifecycle decisions; renewal boxes contain only ideas nobody is resourced to try; the group leaves without a decision to stop anything.

## Choosing between overlapping structures

- **TRIZ vs. inversion / reverse brainstorming.** TRIZ is the facilitated social version: the group generates failure, votes, and then checks its own behaviour. Use TRIZ when the blocker is collective and unspoken; use inversion when one analyst needs a rigorous failure analysis.
- **Wicked Questions vs. six thinking hats.** Hats separate modes of thinking about one question; wicked questions preserve a tension between two true commitments. Use hats to evaluate, wicked questions to frame.
- **Ecocycle vs. options-portfolio.** Options-portfolio balances a set of bets by type and risk; Ecocycle adds lifecycle and forces a stopping decision. Use Ecocycle when the portfolio is old, crowded, or protected.
- **1-2-4-All vs. brainwriting.** Both protect independent thought. 1-2-4-All is faster, synchronous, and ends in shared themes; brainwriting goes deeper with multiple build passes. Use 1-2-4-All for a meeting, brainwriting for a campaign.
